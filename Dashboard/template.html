Guide

What every number on the site means and how it's built. Every model here is checked against past seasons before it's used.

Key terms
Player Impact
How much a player has changed his team's goal differential this season, compared with the same team when he's off the ice, in his actual minutes. Shown in goals or in standings points (2 points = 1 win).
Impact per 60 and windows
Impact divided by minutes on ice, per 60 minutes: what a player produced per minute, so a depth player who makes the most of 9 minutes can be compared with a top-liner. On the Players tab, pick the window: the season, or his team's last 20, 10 or 5 games (the sum of his per-game Impact in those games). Percentiles compare qualified players at the same position across the NHL. Unlike Talent, nothing is pooled or pulled toward average, so it describes this stretch, not how good he is.
Qualified
At least half a regular's minutes over his team's games in the window: 7.5 minutes per team game for forwards, 9.5 for defensemen, 30 for goalies. The floor grows with the window and through the season (10 team games: 75 minutes for a forward). It keeps tiny samples from topping the per-60 lists, but even qualified samples are noisy: the page shows how much of the per-60 spread is real at the median qualified sample, from the model's reliability (half the spread is real at about 630 minutes for forwards, 960 for defensemen; goalies need far more).
Talent
How good a player is, regardless of ice time: his Impact pooled over this season and up to three earlier ones, pulled toward average when the sample is small, adjusted for age, and stated at a standard workload so a 4th-liner and a 1st-liner are compared on equal minutes.
Standings points vs. average
The common unit for players and teams: how many standings points a player or team adds over an average one across a season (84 games).
Likely range
Where the true value probably sits given the sample: the dark bar is 50%, the light bar 90%. Early in a season ranges are wide.
Tier
Groups players or teams the data can't confidently tell apart. A new tier starts when the tier's best is better than the next player or team with 75% probability.
Expected goals (xG)
Shot quality: how often an NHL shot from that spot and type goes in.
Elite skaters / the playoff floor
Skaters in the top 10% of talent at their position, among a team's top 12 forwards and top 6 defensemen by ice time. In past playoffs the first two mattered most, and every Cup winner since 2014 had at least two.
Playoff strength
A team's chance of beating an average playoff team in a seven-game series.
Win % vs. average
Chance of beating a perfectly average team on neutral ice.
Confirmed / leaning
A team strength or weakness is "confirmed" once the data clears 95% confidence after accounting for sample size and how much the stat carries over, "leaning" at about 85%.
Deserve-to-win
Every shot in a game replayed 20,000 times at its expected-goal value: who had the better chances, not who played harder. Empty-net shots excluded.
Player calibers and goalie labels

Skaters are grouped by talent percentile at their position: Superstar (top 2%), Elite (top 10%), Top of lineup (top third), Middle (middle third), Depth (bottom third), Fringe (bottom 10%). Each step is a real one: a player at the top of a group beats one at the top of the next group down about 80% of the time.

Goalies are compared with the league's 32 likely starters, because what matters is clearing a floor, not climbing a ladder: Below starter floor (bottom 20% of starters), Borderline starter (20th-40th), Starter quality (above the 40th). Over 12 seasons, teams with a bottom-20% starter lost about 14 goals a season beyond what their skaters predicted; above the 40th percentile, more goalie talent added nothing measurable, in the regular season or the playoffs. Goalie ratings are noisy, so a goalie near a line is uncertain.

Players who haven't played this season are rated from earlier seasons and marked "no games". Brand-new players start from a below-average baseline that rises with NHL experience.

Power rankings

The rating is a team's expected goal differential for the rest of the season. It blends four things with weights that shift as the season goes (fitted and tested on 8 past seasons): the talent of the lineup each team has dressed in its last 5 games, at the minutes they played; goal differential so far; shot quality so far; and last season's results. Early in the season, lineup talent and last season carry most of the weight. The four "where it comes from" columns show each part in standings points per 84 games.

Likely rank = where the team ranks in 80% of simulations. Tiers start where a tier's top team is better than a team with at least 75% probability; early-season tiers are broad. Top impact players = the three skaters with the most expected impact in their role. G = the likely starter and backup, each rated as if he starts.

Team style tags describe how a team plays relative to the league (see Style tags).

Game odds use the power ratings plus home ice (about a quarter of a goal a game), rest (a team on the second night of a back-to-back loses some win probability) and each team's recent goalie mix. Who starts in goal moves odds less than you'd expect, usually under a point of win probability. Expected points: win = 2, overtime or shootout loss = 1.

Tested and not adopted: replaying last season with this season's roster, roster continuity, a goalie floor, and other lineup windows or minute weightings. None beat the current model.

Lineup strength

Each team's best possible lineup by talent, built the way real lines are: every line needs a center (at least two in the top six forwards, four in the twelve), centers can play wing, and wingers are interchangeable. Top = best six forwards + top four defensemen; Depth = the next six forwards + the third pair. Built from talent, not this week's deployment, so it shows what each roster could ice. Unlike the power rankings, every player counts equally. A 7th defenseman dressed as a forward fills the 12th forward slot.

Playoff odds

Every remaining regular-season game is simulated 10,000 times with the power ratings, then the playoffs are played out under the NHL format (top three in each division plus two wild cards per conference; the better division winner meets the second wild card). Each series is decided by the series model below. Each team's simulated strength is the same draw in the regular season and the playoffs.

Series model. Power rating plus elite skaters. Two versions predicted past series about equally well once the choice was tested fairly: counting every elite skater, or counting only the first two. They differ most for teams with zero elite skaters, where history is thinnest (14 series), so the site uses the average of the two. The series edge is then cut to 70% of its fitted size, the setting that best matched past results from early in the season.

How sure: making the playoffs has clear skill (season simulation, 8 seasons); round one has good skill; later rounds and the Cup have only modest skill, and each season's early favorite has rarely won. Read Cup odds as low-confidence.

Needs, possible moves and fits (Team tab)

Skills are summed over the players a team has been dressing, weighted by ice time; driving play and defense use the team's own 5v5 chance rates. A need = clearly below the league (about the bottom quarter). Possible moves = extras outside the best lineup by talent, or players clearly overpaid for their output. Fits = players elsewhere who cover a need: talent gain is his talent minus the player he'd replace; "covers" = need areas where he's at the 70th percentile or better. "Sellers" are judged on today's playoff odds, which mean little before about 20 games. A screen, not a trade forecast: the data has no contract term, trade clauses, real cap space, injuries, prospects or team intentions.

Strengths and weaknesses (Team tab)

Every team stat ranked against the league this season. A verdict appears only when the data clears 95% ("confirmed") or about 85% ("leaning"), after accounting for sample size and how much each stat carries over from one stretch of games to the next.

Season outlook (Team tab)

10,000 simulations of every remaining game from the power ratings, home ice and rest. The chart shows the spread of final points; the line is this season's simulated playoff line in the team's conference. These projections beat "current pace" at every point of the season in 8 past seasons.

Matchup

Why the odds lean breaks a game's odds into its parts, from 50-50: home ice, rest, roster talent, last season, chances and goals this season, and the goalies picked. Bars point toward the team each part helps.

How each team wins uses only stats that carry over from game to game: 5v5 chances, slot shots, power play and penalty kill, penalties, and goalie talent. Faceoffs add nothing measurable once chances are counted, and hits are a style with no effect on winning in our tests. Each team's rates pool this season with last season at half weight. "Running hot or cold" (finishing and save results) barely predicts the next games.

Likely to score = each player's shot quality per minute (this season and three before, shrunk toward typical) x expected ice time x how many chances the opponent allows, with finishing heavily regressed; checked against this season's games.

Expected box score = each team's per-game rates adjusted for the opponent. During a game, a team well above its expected-goals line is playing to its edge even if it's losing.

Live win probability lets goals arrive at each team's expected rate (calibrated so 0-0 at puck drop equals the pre-game odds), with overtime at the model's odds. It ignores score effects and empty nets, so late leads are slightly safer than shown. Not tested on in-game data.

Lines come from each team's latest game (Utah: current lineup). Line value = the players' summed talent. Coaches don't match lines exactly, so read it as where the depth edges are.

Roster

Impact = what a player has contributed this season in his actual minutes; Talent = how good he is at a standard workload. Bars show the likely range (dark 50%, light 90%). "Clearly above/below average" appears only when this season's Impact is outside the 95% range of chance and he has 250+ minutes. Team rank orders all positions by talent (F1 = best forward).

Game by game: best and worst three by Impact in each game, from MoneyPuck game data.

Style tags (players and teams)

Style tags say what stands out about how a player or team plays compared with the whole league, not how good they are. Each tag has to be a real habit: tested on 14 past seasons, a tag is used only if most players (or teams) who get it still show that trait the next season.

  • Players: this season plus the previous two (weighted 1, 0.7, 0.4), per-60 rates pulled toward the position average by ice time, compared with forwards or defensemen. Each tag's cut is the loosest at which at least 55% of tagged players are still top-20% at it next season (chance: 20%): Shooter, Playmaker (top 20%); Physical, Shot blocker, Puck thief, Draws penalties (top 15%); Play driver (top 10%, 5-on-5). Usage tags (Penalty killer, Power-play weapon, Faceoff ace) come from his deployment.
  • Weak spot (red, at most one): Penalty-prone, Low shot threat, Gets out-chanced, each repeating next season at least half the time.
  • Best trait (dashed): when no trait clears the bar, his strongest one and its percentile, so it reads as a relative strength, not a style.
  • Dropped because they mostly don't repeat: Finisher (scoring above shot quality: at best 49% repeat) and Shutdown (chances against with him on: 42%).
  • Teams: this season plus last season at half weight (mostly last season until about 15 games), top or bottom ~20% of the league. Kept tags repeat 45-67% of the time. How good a team is at defense, special teams, finishing or goaltending lives in Strengths and Weaknesses instead; Quality over quantity, Protects the slot, Finishing team, Goaltending-driven and Strong penalty kill were dropped as too noisy (28-41%). "Closest to" names a team's most distinctive trait when none clears the bar.
Player profiles

How a player has played, not how good he is (that's talent). The player type sums up his role by ice time plus the type his skills and usage point to. Skills are his percentile among NHL forwards or defensemen on each part of Player Impact (faceoffs: centers only), pooled over up to four seasons and pulled toward average by how much each skill repeats year to year. Takeaways vs. giveaways: players who carry the puck a lot give it away more, so stars can rank low there. Usage percentiles describe, they don't grade.

Goalie profiles show shot-stopping by danger level, rebounds and workload, pooled over up to four seasons and pulled hard toward average, since goalie numbers are the noisiest in hockey. "Talent as a starter" is what he'd add over a full starter's workload.

Value vs. contract

In his role = what he's expected to deliver in the minutes he actually plays (talent x ice time): what the team gets for the money. Raw talent = how good he is at a standard workload. Both are compared with what his cap hit buys on the open market, from a line fitted across every NHL skater's 2026-27 cap hit. ELC = entry-level contract, cheap by rule, so those players look like bargains. A value is colored only when its whole 90% range is on one side of zero. Goalies aren't valued (their talent is too uncertain for a fair price). Cap hits: Spotrac.

Games

Every game of the season, browsable by date (the arrows skip days without games; the team filter shows one team's games). Away team on top, home team below.

  • Finished games: score, deserve-to-win (every shot replayed 20,000 times at its expected-goal value; empty-net shots left out), shots and chances, goalies, every shot on a rink, and Player Impact in this game for both teams: the full Impact model run on that game alone, so a player is compared with his own team in that same game. Where it came from splits each bar into the same parts as the season bars (each measured against the average forward or defenseman): positives stack right of zero, negatives left, and the tick marks the total. Skaters set the scale; a goalie's saves vs. expected can run off the end. One game is mostly noise (about half a goal of uncertainty for a skater), so use it to see who drove a night, not to judge a player.
  • Upcoming games: win odds from the power ratings, home ice and rest (a team on the second night of a back-to-back loses some win probability), as of the morning update, plus how the two teams compare and the season series.
  • Odds before the game: from version 1.4.0 on, each morning's odds are saved, so finished games show what the model expected beforehand, and an upset tag marks a winner the model gave under 40%. Earlier games have none; filling them in now would use results the model didn't have then.
  • Standouts (▲) and rough nights (▼) replace a fixed top and bottom three. The standout percentile ranks a night among every forward, defenseman or goalie night this season, measured against his own teammates that night: his game Impact minus his team's skater average (goalies: saves vs. expected, which doesn't depend on teammates' scoring). A standout is top 10% with a positive game; a rough night, bottom 10% with a negative one: about 2 per team per game, and about 1 team-game in 8 has none (top 15% named about 3 a team almost every night, so the label stopped meaning much; top 5% left 42% of team-games empty). No cap: a night with six standouts shows six; when nobody qualifies, the closest player is named.
    Why against teammates: raw game values lean with the score (a team's summed skater values track that game's goal differential, r 0.67), so without it a 6-1 win made most winners "standouts" and the losers "rough nights". That's also why a player's percentile and his raw goals can rank players differently: +0.84 goals on a night his team averaged +0.15 is a smaller standout than +0.60 on a night it averaged -0.20.
    The Impact bars can be ordered by raw Impact (the default: what each player added in goals) or by standout percentile; the share graphic follows that choice. A stricter "statistically clear" rule was rejected: one game is too small a sample, so it would flag fewer than one player per team per game.
  • A game's player values appear once MoneyPuck posts its game-by-game data, usually by the next morning.
Game recap and preview graphics

Recaps show the score, deserve-to-win, the expected-goals race, the numbers, every player's Impact in the game for both teams (bars, best to worst), and the game-winner (win chance through the game uses league-average scoring for both teams, so it ignores team strength). The Games tab draws the recap for any finished game from either team's point of view. Previews match the Matchup tab, including any goalies you pick. Team colors only, no logos.
